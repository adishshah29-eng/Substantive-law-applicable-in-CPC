Beneficiaries of an employees' welfare trust sought to amend their
pleadings in a suit against the trustees concerning mismanagement of
trust assets. The trial court refused the amendment; the High Court
allowed it; the trustees appealed to the Supreme Court.

The Supreme Court held that Order VI Rule 17 of the Code of Civil
Procedure, 1908 comprises two distinct parts: the first part, which
confers a general discretion on the court to allow either party to
alter or amend its pleadings at any stage of the proceedings, is
discretionary; the second part, which requires that all amendments
"necessary for the purpose of determining the real questions in
controversy between the parties" shall be allowed, is not discretionary
but obligatory -- courts are enjoined to allow such amendments, and
refusal is permissible only where the amendment would work serious
injustice or irremediable prejudice to the other side, or introduce a
totally different, new and inconsistent case, or that the effect of the
amendment would be to take away from the other side a legal right which
had accrued in its favour, such as a bar of limitation. Applying this
test, the Court held that the proposed amendment merely clarified and
elaborated the existing claim concerning mismanagement of trust
property, and did not introduce an inconsistent new cause of action, and
accordingly upheld its allowance.

Rajesh Kumar Aggarwal v K.K. Modi is a leading authority for the
distinction between the discretionary and mandatory limbs of Order VI
Rule 17 CPC, and for the liberal approach courts must take toward
amendments that are genuinely necessary to determine the real
controversy between the parties.
