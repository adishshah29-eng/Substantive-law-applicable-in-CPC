The purchaser sued for specific performance of an agreement to purchase a
flat. By the time the matter reached the Supreme Court, the value of the
property had risen very substantially since the date of the agreement.
The defendant argued that specific performance should be refused, or
that the plaintiff should be put to terms reflecting the increase in
value, given the delay and price escalation.

The Supreme Court held that mere escalation in the price of the subject
property, by itself, is not a sufficient ground to deny a decree of
specific performance to a plaintiff who has otherwise established her
entitlement and has been ready and willing to perform. The Court
observed that specific performance is a discretionary and equitable
remedy, and that discretion can be exercised by granting specific
performance while simultaneously imposing reasonable additional
conditions on the plaintiff -- for instance, requiring payment of a
further sum reflecting part of the escalation -- as a term of the decree,
rather than refusing the decree outright merely because prices have
risen.

Nirmala Anand is cited for the proposition that price escalation between
the date of agreement and the date of decree is not, without more,
grounds to refuse specific performance; courts may instead balance equity
by moulding the terms of the decree.
