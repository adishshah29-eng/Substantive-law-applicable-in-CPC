The plaintiffs sought specific performance of an agreement of sale where
they had defaulted in paying successive instalments on the stipulated
dates, and sought to pay the balance and complete the sale several years
later, relying on the settled position that time is not of the essence
in contracts for sale of immovable property.

The Supreme Court, while not overruling the general rule, held that the
rule evolved in a very different social and economic context and that
changed circumstances -- in particular, the phenomenon of rapid and
continuous escalation in the price of immovable property -- require
courts to be more cautious in granting specific performance to a
plaintiff who has been consistently and repeatedly in default of the
payment schedule fixed by the contract. The Court signalled that the
traditional rule may need reconsideration and, in appropriate cases with
prolonged, unexplained default, discretionary specific performance may
properly be refused in favour of a compensation-based remedy, particularly
where the vendor stands to be prejudiced by continuing price rise
attributable to the purchaser's default.

Saradamani Kandappan is read alongside K.S. Vidyanadam as tempering the
older, more categorical application of the "time not of the essence"
principle in immovable property contracts, in light of realistic
economic conditions, in the exercise of the (then still discretionary)
jurisdiction to grant specific performance.
