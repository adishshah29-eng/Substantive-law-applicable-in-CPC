The trial court had refused to take a belatedly filed written statement
on record, treating the ninety-day outer limit under the proviso to
Order VIII Rule 1 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 as an absolute
bar, and proceeded to decide the suit without considering the
defendant's defence at all.

The Supreme Court, reaffirming the line of authority beginning with
Kailash v Nanhku and Salem Advocate Bar Association, and as further
explained in R.N. Jadi & Brothers, reiterated that the time limit in
Order VIII Rule 1 is directory and not mandatory, and that a trial court
retains discretion to take a written statement on record beyond ninety
days in a deserving case, for reasons to be recorded, rather than
mechanically shutting out a defendant's defence. The Court cautioned,
however, that this discretion is an exception and not the norm, to be
exercised only where the delay is satisfactorily explained and refusal
would result in real injustice, and remanded the matter for the trial
court to consider the belated written statement on its merits rather
than rejecting it outright on the ground of delay alone.

Desh Raj v Rohtash Singh is a recent reaffirmation of the settled
position -- traceable to Kailash v Nanhku -- that the ninety-day limit
in Order VIII Rule 1 CPC is directory, and that outright refusal to take
a delayed written statement on record without considering the reasons
for delay is not a proper exercise of the court's discretion.
