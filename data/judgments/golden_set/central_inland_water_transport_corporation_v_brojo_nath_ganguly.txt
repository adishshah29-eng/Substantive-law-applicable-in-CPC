A government-owned corporation's service rules contained a clause
permitting termination of a permanent employee's service without
assigning any reason, on three months' notice or pay in lieu, at the
sole discretion of management. Employees terminated under this clause
challenged it as void.

The Supreme Court held that an unfair and unreasonable clause in a
contract entered into between parties of grossly unequal bargaining
power -- where one party has no meaningful choice but to accept the
terms dictated by the other -- can be struck down as opposed to public
policy under Section 23 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, even where no
recognised head of public policy squarely covers the case. The Court held
that Indian courts have the same wide jurisdiction as English courts to
declare a contract or a term of a contract to be void as against public
policy, and that this jurisdiction is not confined to the narrow, closed
categories historically recognised. Since the impugned service rule gave
the employer an unrestrained power to terminate a permanent employee's
services arbitrarily, without any reason and without a hearing, it was
held unconscionable and unreasonable and therefore void under Section 23.

The judgment established that gross inequality of bargaining power,
combined with a term that is unreasonable and unfair to the weaker party,
can itself render that term void as opposed to public policy under
Section 23 of the Contract Act, independent of any statutory or
constitutional infirmity.
