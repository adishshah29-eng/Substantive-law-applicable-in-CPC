The respondents sought a temporary injunction under Order XXXIX Rules 1
and 2 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 restraining the appellants
from interfering with their possession of certain agricultural land
pending disposal of the suit. The trial court and the High Court granted
the injunction; the appellants challenged the grant before the Supreme
Court.

The Supreme Court held that satisfaction of the court, exercising
discretion judicially under Order XXXIX Rules 1 and 2, that all three of
the following conditions coexist is a precondition to the grant of a
temporary injunction: (i) the applicant must make out a prima facie
case in support of the right claimed; (ii) the balance of convenience
must be in favour of the applicant, in the sense that greater injury
would result from withholding the injunction than from granting it; and
(iii) the applicant must show that he would suffer irreparable injury if
the injunction is not granted, which cannot be adequately compensated in
damages. The Court emphasised that these three requirements are
conjunctive, not disjunctive -- an applicant who establishes a prima
facie case but fails to show either that the balance of convenience
favours him or that he faces irreparable injury is not entitled to a
temporary injunction. The Court explained that "prima facie case" does
not mean that the plaintiff must show a case that will certainly succeed
at trial; it means the existence of a serious question to be tried,
with a probability that the plaintiff is entitled to the relief claimed,
on the material placed before the court at that stage.

Dalpat Kumar v Prahlad Singh is the leading and most frequently cited
authority for the proposition that prima facie case, balance of
convenience, and irreparable injury are three independent and cumulative
requirements for a temporary injunction under Order XXXIX Rules 1 and 2
CPC, and that satisfaction of only one or two of them, without the third,
does not justify the grant of an injunction.
