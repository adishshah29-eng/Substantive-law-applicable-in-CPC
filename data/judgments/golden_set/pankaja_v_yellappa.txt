An application to amend the plaint in a partition suit, seeking to add a
prayer for possession consequential upon the declaration of title already
claimed, was rejected by the trial court and the High Court on the view
that it introduced a new relief at a late stage.

The Supreme Court reiterated the settled principles governing amendment
of pleadings under Order VI Rule 17 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908:
courts should allow amendments that are necessary for determining the
real controversy between the parties, and should refuse amendments only
where they change the fundamental character of the suit, cause serious
prejudice that cannot be undone by an award of costs, or seek to
withdraw an admission or take away a right that has accrued to the other
side by lapse of time. The Court held that seeking a consequential relief
of possession, flowing naturally from a declaration of title already in
issue in the suit, does not alter the fundamental nature of the suit and
ought ordinarily to be allowed, since disallowing it would only lead to a
fresh suit for possession and consequent multiplicity of proceedings --
precisely what Order VI Rule 17 and the liberal approach to amendment
are intended to avoid.

Pankaja v Yellappa is cited for the principle that amendments seeking
relief consequential upon, and arising naturally out of, the existing
pleadings should ordinarily be permitted to avoid multiplicity of
litigation, applying the broader test laid down for Order VI Rule 17
CPC.
