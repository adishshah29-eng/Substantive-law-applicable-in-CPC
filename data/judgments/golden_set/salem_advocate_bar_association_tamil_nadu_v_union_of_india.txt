Following the sweeping amendments made to the Code of Civil Procedure,
1908 by the Amendment Acts of 1999 and 2002 -- including the insertion of
a time limit for filing the written statement under Order VIII Rule 1,
restrictions on the number of adjournments, and mandatory reference to
alternative dispute resolution under Section 89 -- their constitutional
validity was challenged before the Supreme Court, and a committee was
constituted to examine practical difficulties in implementation.

This second Salem Advocate Bar Association judgment, considering the
committee's report, upheld the constitutional validity of the amendments,
including the time limit for filing a written statement under Order VIII
Rule 1. However, consistently with the object of the amendments being to
expedite civil litigation without causing injustice, the Court clarified
that the time limits introduced by the 1999/2002 amendments -- including
the ninety-day outer limit for filing the written statement -- are to be
understood as ordinarily directory, giving courts residual discretion to
extend time in a given case for good and sufficient reasons, rather than
as rigid, inflexible bars whose breach automatically forecloses a
defendant's right to be heard, so as to avoid the amendments themselves
becoming an instrument of injustice.

Salem Advocate Bar Association (II) is the leading authority upholding
the 1999/2002 CPC amendments as constitutionally valid, while
construing the time limits they introduced, including that in Order
VIII Rule 1, as directory safeguards for expeditious disposal rather than
mandatory, non-extendable bars -- a construction subsequently applied and
elaborated in Kailash v Nanhku.
