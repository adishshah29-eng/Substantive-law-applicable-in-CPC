An old lady gifted certain land to her daughter, subject to a direction
in the same transaction that the daughter pay an annuity to the donor's
sister (the plaintiff). The daughter executed a separate instrument in
the sister's favour agreeing to pay the annuity, but later refused to
pay, contending that the plaintiff had furnished no consideration for
that promise and was a stranger to the consideration.

The Madras High Court held that while under the Indian Contract Act,
1872 a stranger to a contract generally cannot sue upon it, a stranger to
the consideration may sue if she is a party to the contract itself. Here
the gift deed and the annuity instrument formed parts of the same
transaction, and the consideration for the daughter's promise to pay the
annuity had in substance moved from the donor (who gave up the land on
that condition), even though the benefit of the promise ran to the
plaintiff. Section 2(d) of the Contract Act defines consideration
broadly enough to permit consideration to move from a third person, so
long as it moves at the desire of the promisor; it need not move from the
promisee.

Chinnaya v Ramayya is cited as the leading Indian authority establishing
that, unlike the stricter English common law rule, Indian contract law
permits consideration for a promise to be furnished by a person other
than the promisee, provided the other requirements of a valid contract
are satisfied.
