In an election petition, the returned candidate's written statement was
filed beyond the ninety-day outer limit prescribed by the proviso to
Order VIII Rule 1 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, as it then stood
following the 1999/2002 amendments. The question was whether the proviso
was mandatory, so that a written statement filed even one day beyond
ninety days from service of summons must be rejected and the defendant
treated as if no defence had been filed at all.

The Supreme Court held that the provisions of Order VIII Rule 1,
prescribing the period within which the written statement must be
presented, are a part of the procedural law and are, therefore, ordinarily
directory and not mandatory, notwithstanding that the language of the
proviso appears imperative. The Court held that procedural law is meant
to subserve and facilitate the cause of justice and not to govern or
obstruct it, and that a provision of this kind, though couched in
mandatory-sounding language, should be construed as directory unless a
contrary legislative intention is clearly discernible, particularly
where a rigid, mandatory construction would result in grave injustice to
a party without correspondingly serving the object of the provision.
Accordingly, the Court held that a court retains the discretion, in
appropriate cases and for sufficient cause shown, to extend the time for
filing a written statement even beyond the ninety-day outer limit fixed
by the proviso to Order VIII Rule 1, though ordinarily such extension
beyond ninety days should be granted only in exceptional circumstances,
for detailed and cogent reasons, and not as a matter of course.

Kailash v Nanhku is the leading authority establishing that the
ninety-day time limit in the proviso to Order VIII Rule 1 CPC is
directory rather than an absolute, non-extendable mandatory bar, though
courts must exercise the power to extend sparingly and for compelling
reasons.
