The plaintiffs sued for specific performance of an agreement to sell
immovable property several years after the agreed date for completion,
during a period of rapidly rising land prices. The agreement did not
expressly make time of the essence, and the plaintiffs relied on the
traditional rule that, in contracts for sale of immovable property, time
is presumed not to be of the essence.

The Supreme Court held that while the general rule that time is not of
the essence in contracts for sale of immovable property remains good
law, courts exercising the discretionary jurisdiction under the Specific
Relief Act, 1963 to grant specific performance must take into account the
realities of the situation, including inordinate, unexplained delay by
the plaintiff and steep escalation in property prices in the interregnum.
Such delay and its consequences are relevant circumstances going to the
equity of granting the discretionary relief of specific performance, and
a court may, on that basis, decline specific performance and instead
award compensation, even though the contract does not make time strictly
of the essence.

K.S. Vidyanadam qualified the older, more mechanical application of "time
is not of the essence" in immovable property contracts, holding that
unexplained delay coupled with market conditions can properly weigh
against a decree of specific performance in the court's discretion,
though it did not disturb the basic proposition that specific performance
of contracts for sale of immovable property was, prior to the 2018
amendment, a discretionary and not automatic remedy.
