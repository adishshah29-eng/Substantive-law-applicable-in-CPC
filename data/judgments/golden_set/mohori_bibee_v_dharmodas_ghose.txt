This appeal from the Calcutta High Court arose out of a mortgage executed by
the respondent, Dharmodas Ghose, in favour of Brahmo Dutt, a moneylender,
while the respondent was still a minor. The mortgagee's attorney had
actual knowledge of the respondent's minority at the time the deed was
executed. The respondent, through his mother as guardian, sued to have
the mortgage declared void.

The Judicial Committee of the Privy Council held that under Section 11 of
the Indian Contract Act, 1872, a person is competent to contract only if
he has attained the age of majority; consequently an agreement entered
into by a minor is not merely voidable but void ab initio. The Board
rejected the argument that Section 10 and Section 11, read together,
merely rendered such a contract voidable at the minor's option. Their
Lordships observed that the Contract Act makes it essential that all
contracting parties should be competent to contract, and a minor is not
competent; the case is not therefore a case of a contract voidable at the
option of the minor, but of one in which there never was, and could not
be, any contract at all.

The Board further held that since there was no contract in the eye of
law, the provisions of Sections 64 and 65 of the Contract Act -- which
deal with the consequences of rescission or discovery that an agreement
is void, as between competent parties -- had no application to a case
where one party was always incompetent to contract. The minor could
therefore not be compelled to repay the money advanced to him under the
void mortgage, notwithstanding that the mortgagee had acted in good faith
and without notice at the inception of the transaction (a point on which
the record in fact showed the mortgagee's agent had notice of the
minority).

This case remains the foundational authority in Indian law for the
proposition that agreements with minors are void, not merely voidable,
and that the ordinary doctrine of restitution does not operate to revive
an obligation that never had legal existence.
