The defendant company agreed to sell plots of land to the plaintiff's
predecessor, undertaking to develop roads and drains and complete the
work within a reasonable time, with completion of the sale conditional
on that development. A portion of the land was requisitioned by the
Government for military purposes during the Second World War, which the
defendant claimed rendered the contract impossible of performance and
frustrated under Section 56 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872.

The Supreme Court held that the doctrine of frustration in Indian law is
not a branch of the English common law doctrine of frustration but is
covered directly by the express terms of Section 56, which states that a
contract to do an act that, after the contract is made, becomes
impossible, or, by reason of some event the promisor could not prevent,
unlawful, becomes void. The word "impossible" in Section 56 is not used
in the strict physical sense; it includes impracticability and useless
performance from the point of view of the object and purpose the parties
had in mind. However, mere temporary delay or hardship in performance,
falling short of the venture becoming impracticable or the object
frustrated, does not attract Section 56.

On the facts, the Court held that the requisition was of a temporary
character and did not go to the root of the contract; the purpose of the
contract -- development and eventual sale of the plots -- had not become
impossible or radically different, merely delayed. The contract therefore
was not frustrated and the buyer's suit for specific performance and
possession was not barred.

Satyabrata Ghose is the leading authority establishing that Section 56 of
the Contract Act is a complete and self-contained code for supervening
impossibility in India, and that impossibility under that section extends
to impracticability of the very foundation of the contract, not merely
literal physical impossibility.
